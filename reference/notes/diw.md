Direct ink writing is a niche process. You reach for it when formulation is critical to function: ceramic pastes, colloidal systems, writable electronics, and more recently tissue scaffolds, where many academic groups are active [1–3]. Some use it for drug delivery, where the ability to vary the formulation matters [4].

There is no universal good ink; it depends on the end application. Rheology is always the deciding property: the ink has to flow through the nozzle under pressure, then hold its shape once deposited. The user owns that materials problem.

Fugitive inks show the approach well. At UIUC, they were printed and then removed to build three-dimensional microfluidic networks [5].

I expect DIW to stay limited in reach, but valuable in highly specialized areas.
